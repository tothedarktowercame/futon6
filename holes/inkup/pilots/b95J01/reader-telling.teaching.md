# b95J01 reader-oriented telling

## Teaching

**[B0]** Let \(G\) be a group and \(H\) a subgroup of finite index. We prove
three things: for every \(g\in G\) there is a least positive \(k\) with
\(g^{k}\in H\), and it divides every \(m\) with \(g^{m}\in H\); if \(H\) is
normal then \(k\) divides the index \([G:H]\); and without normality that
divisibility can fail, which we show in \(S_{3}\).

**[B1]** The structural idea is to package the exponents as one set:
\(T=\{m\in\mathbb{Z}: g^{m}\in H\}\). You could instead take a least positive
exponent by well-ordering and then run the division algorithm to see every
other exponent is a multiple of it, handling negative exponents separately;
that works, but \(T\) does all of it at once. The map \(m\mapsto g^{m}\) is a
homomorphism \(\mathbb{Z}\to G\), and \(T\) is the preimage of \(H\) under
it, so \(T\) is a subgroup of \(\mathbb{Z}\): one statement about \(T\)
delivers the least positive element and divisibility for all integers,
negative ones included.

**[B2]** Next, \(T\) must contain something positive, and this is the only
place finite index enters part (a). Write \(n=[G:H]\) and list the \(n+1\)
cosets \(H=g^{0}H,g^{1}H,\dots,g^{n}H\). There are only \(n\) cosets in all,
so two of the listed ones coincide: \(g^{i}H=g^{j}H\) with
\(0\le i<j\le n\). Multiplying on the left by \(g^{-i}\) gives \(g^{j-i}H=H\), that is \(g^{j-i}\in H\) with
\(j-i>0\), a pigeonhole pairing worth being able to reproduce. Without finite
index nothing of the sort needs to happen: \(g\) of infinite order with \(H\)
trivial has no positive exponent in \(H\) at all, so the least \(k\) would
not exist.

**[B3]** Since \(T\) is a subgroup of \(\mathbb{Z}\) containing the positive
integer \(j-i\), it is nonzero, and every subgroup of \(\mathbb{Z}\) is
cyclic, generated by its least positive element (this is where the division
algorithm lives): \(T=a\mathbb{Z}\) for a single \(a\ne0\).

**[B4]** Put \(k=|a|\). Membership in \(T=a\mathbb{Z}\) says exactly that
\(a\) divides \(m\), so for every integer \(m\), positive or negative,
\(g^{m}\in H\) if and only if \(k\mid m\).

**[B5]** Part (a) falls out: \(k=|a|\) is positive, \(g^{k}\in H\) by the
criterion at \(m=k\), it is least because any positive \(l\) with
\(g^{l}\in H\) has \(k\mid l\) hence \(k\le l\), and it divides every
\(m\) with \(g^{m}\in H\).

**[B6]** Now suppose \(H\) is normal. The cosets then form the quotient
group \(G/H\), of order \([G:H]\), and this upgrade is the hinge of part
(b). The coset \(gH\) has order exactly \(k\): its powers are
\(g^{j}H\), and \(g^{j}H=H\) precisely when \(g^{j}\in H\), which by part
(a) happens exactly at multiples of \(k\). You can see the conclusion
either as Lagrange — the order of an element divides the order of the finite
group — or concretely: in a group of order \([G:H]\), every element's
\([G:H]\)-th power is the identity, so \(g^{[G:H]}H=H\), that is
\(g^{[G:H]}\in H\).

**[B7]** Apply the membership criterion of part (a) to the single integer
\(m=[G:H]\): since \(g^{[G:H]}\in H\), we get \(k\mid[G:H]\).

**[B8]** For the counterexample take \(G=S_{3}\) and \(H\) the subgroup
generated by the transposition \((1\,2)\). It has order \(2\) and index
\(3\), and it is not normal: conjugating \((1\,2)\) by \((1\,3)\) gives
\((2\,3)\), a transposition outside \(H\).

**[B9]** Take \(g=(1\,3)\), another transposition. Then \(g\notin H\) but
\(g^{2}=e\in H\), so the least positive exponent is \(k=2\), while the index
is \(3\) and \(2\) does not divide \(3\): the conclusion of part (b) fails
for non-normal \(H\). Note why the first reflex, a \(3\)-cycle, would not
have worked: a \(3\)-cycle and its square lie outside \(H\), so its least positive
exponent is \(3\), which divides the index \(3\); it satisfies the conclusion and
refutes nothing. The counterexample needs an involution against an index-3
subgroup.
